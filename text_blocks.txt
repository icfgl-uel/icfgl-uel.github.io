This paper forms part of a broader doctoral project examining how legal systems categorise individuals on the basis of personal characteristics such as sex/gender, race, and caste, and the consequences of these categorisation exercises for equality, legal recognition, and access to rights. The project studies how law operationalises identity categories, mapping the purposes for which classifications are used and identifying the contradictions, inconsistencies, and ‘fit-related problems’ that arise when rigid legal categories encounter complex, fluid, and lived realities. It asks a broader jurisprudential question central to contemporary equality debates: when does law need to classify, and how should legal systems balance the need for categorisation with the need to minimise its exclusionary harms? This paper focuses on gendered terminology within family law and related statutory frameworks in India, examining how legal categories such as ‘man’, ‘woman’, ‘mother’, ‘father’, ‘wife’, and ‘husband’ accommodate, exclude, or otherwise respond to trans persons. Family law provides a particularly significant site for examining legal gender classification because it regulates intimate relationships, kinship, caregiving, parenthood, succession, and social legitimacy, while simultaneously reflecting deeply embedded assumptions about sex, reproduction, and the family. Legal understandings of motherhood and fatherhood frequently remain tied to biological expectations concerning reproduction, gestation, caregiving, and familial roles. As a result, family law remains one of the strongest sites of gender essentialism within contemporary legal systems, even as wider legal frameworks increasingly recognise gender diversity. Drawing on feminist legal theory and intersectional approaches to law-making, the paper conducts a doctrinal analysis of selected statutory frameworks governing marriage, parenthood, caregiving, inheritance, and family status in India. Particular attention is paid to the implications of constitutional recognition of transgender identity following \*National Legal Services Authority v Union of India\* and subsequent legislative developments. The paper maps how gendered terminology is deployed across family law and identifies tensions between formal commitments to gender diversity and legal provisions that continue to rely upon binary or biologically grounded understandings of sex, parenthood, and familial status. Comparative reference is made to developments in the United Kingdom, including debates surrounding the interpretation of ‘sex’ under the Equality Act 2010 and the implications of \*For Women Scotland Ltd v The Scottish Ministers\*, to illustrate wider challenges faced by legal systems attempting to reconcile competing equality, administrative, and regulatory objectives. The paper argues that the challenges of trans inclusion in family law cannot be reduced to the simple absence of non-binary or transgender categories. Rather, they stem from the deeper architecture of family law itself. While biologically grounded classifications may produce exclusionary outcomes for trans and gender-diverse persons, they may also reflect administrative, evidential, and policy concerns associated with regulating family relationships and allocating legal rights and responsibilities. Rather than advocating the wholesale abolition of gendered legal categories, the paper argues for greater scrutiny of legal categorisation projects and explores how feminist law-making might develop less exclusionary approaches to legislative drafting. It suggests that lawmakers should avoid classification unless necessary, prioritise self-identification where compatible with the regulatory purpose being pursued, and provide clearer justification where more prescriptive approaches are retained.
Viet Nam's declining fertility rates and delayed marriage trends have intensified state concerns over population ageing, future labour shortages, and welfare sustainability. In response, the government has increasingly adopted pronatalist interventions, beginning with target-driven fertility policies in low-fertility regions in 2020 and culminating in the 2025 Population Law, which seeks to raise the national total fertility rate through measures such as cash incentives, extended maternity leave, and priority access to social housing. While reproduction and family-making are often framed as matters of private choice, feminist scholars have long argued that the public/private divide obscures the ways states regulate intimate life and rely upon unpaid reproductive labour to sustain national economies. Particularly, pronatalist interventions – which solely prioritise numerical population targets over reproductive autonomy – raise concerns about coercive nature and unintended consequences for reinforcing patriarchal family norms, erosion of reproductive rights and gender equality, and instrumentalising reproductive bodies in pursuit of national economic and demographic goals. Consequently, population policies with pronatalist objectives risk overlooking structural barriers to family-making and childbearing, as well as obscuring significant amount of unpaid care work performed by women. Informed by Marxist feminist perspectives on the political economy of informal care and integrated with the framework of reproductive justice and feminist legal theory, this paper examines how Viet Nam's Population Law and related legislative documents seek to govern reproductive behaviours through legal, economic, and moral mechanisms while simultaneously relocating care responsibilities to households and women. The paper argues that the Population Law reflects a broader form of reproductive governance in which social welfare policies are mobilised not merely to support families, but to incentivise and discipline desired forms of reproduction in response to the demographic anxieties. In doing so, the state re-politicises the "private" sphere of family-making while continuing to treat care as an individualised and feminised responsibility. Although women's labour force participation in Viet Nam remains high, public care infrastructure and support services remain limited, intensifying the dual burden of productive and reproductive labour disproportionately borne by women. Preliminary analysis suggests three key tensions within the policy framework. First, pronatalist measures reinforce gendered expectations of motherhood and unpaid care work while offering insufficient structural support for the redistribution of reproductive labour. Second, policy discussions pay limited attention to the responsibilities of employers and labour institutions in mitigating work–care conflicts and ensuring maternity protections. Third, the framework privileges heteronormativity and repronormativity, selectively valuing reproduction among socially desirable populations while marginalising other reproductive desires, family arrangements, and life trajectories. By examining the intersections of population governance, labour, and social care policy, this paper reveals the contradictions and exclusions embedded within Viet Nam's pronatalist turn. It argues that demographic challenges cannot be addressed solely through fertility incentives or moral appeals to family responsibility. Instead, a reproductive justice approach requires transforming the structural conditions underpinning care inequalities, recognising the value of reproductive labour, and rethinking the relationship between state responsibility, labour policy, and intimate life beyond the public/private divide.
Across many jurisdictions, digital governance and AI-assisted dispute resolution are increasingly promoted as tools for improving access to justice in domestic violence cases. Yet feminist and socio-legal scholarship questions whether platformized justice systems genuinely enhance protection for survivors or instead reshape which forms of gendered violence become legally visible, evidentially legible, and institutionally actionable. This study explores the adjudication and characteristics of domestic violence divorce cases in China within the context of "smart courts" and digital judicial governance. Based on 328 divorce judgments from the China Online Judgments Database (2023), the study combines feminist socio-legal analysis and empirical methods to assess evidentiary practices, judicial recognition of domestic violence, and case outcomes. The results show that domestic violence is rarely formally recognized (3.89%), with most judgments either ignoring or dismissing the allegations. Victims often face severe evidentiary limitations; only 22.96% of victims submitted supporting evidence, primarily informal materials such as photos and chat logs, rather than formal documents. Even when domestic violence is recognized, it does not always lead to divorce, compensation, or strong protections. Instead, case outcomes are largely influenced by repeated litigation, procedural screening, and compromise-oriented child custody arrangements. According to the findings, the trend of digital judicial governance does not appear to have significantly impacted the traditional logic of family law adjudication. Instead, it may have reinforced existing stability-oriented practices, making the handling of domestic violence cases more procedurally efficient, but not substantively protective. The paper argues that digital governance may enhance institutional responsiveness, but such improvement is selective rather than uniform. Platformization can facilitate earlier reporting, faster referral, inter-agency coordination, and the accumulation of standardized evidence, thereby increasing the procedural visibility of certain cases within judicial systems. However, it may also accelerate diversion into mediation-oriented pathways, where domestic violence is reframed as family conflict rather than structural abuse, potentially narrowing access to formal remedies. More fundamentally, digital systems tend to privilege harms that are easily recorded and categorized—such as physical injuries, repeated police contact, or traceable digital communication—while marginalizing coercive control, psychological abuse, and economic domination, which are difficult to translate into standardized evidentiary forms. In this sense, digitalized governance expands procedural access without resolving deeper evidentiary and interpretive constraints in domestic violence adjudication. Overall, the value of digital governance in domestic violence adjudication should not be assessed solely through efficiency or coordination metrics. Instead, it must be evaluated in terms of how technological infrastructures reshape the legal intelligibility and epistemic visibility of gendered harm. While digital systems may expand procedural access, they risk reproducing structural blind spots in recognizing non-physical and coercive forms of domestic violence.
Vietnam will become an aged society by 2036 (UNFPA), with demand for elder, child and disability care rising sharply. Across Vietnamese households, that care is overwhelmingly performed by women under entrenched gender norms. Yet Vietnamese law treats this care as a private family matter, leaving its costs to fall, by default, on women who already perform it. Feminist legal scholarship has long shown that unpaid care is labour, not private duty; the public/private divide that Vietnamese law inscribes around the family withdraws women's care from the categories of legal claim. Four statutes regulate family care without recognising it as work. The Law on Marriage and Family 2014 and the Law on the Elderly 2009 impose care duties without state support; the Law on Social Insurance 2024 excludes care time from pension entitlement; and Article 18 of the Law on Gender Equality 2006 affirms equal spousal responsibility without reaching intra-household labour. These rules are gender-neutral, yet the burden falls on women. Adjacent rules mark women as the default carer \- still-lower retirement age, longer maternity leave, custody of children under 36 months after divorce (Art. 81\) \- while women's lower earnings under the “husband-breadwinner” model make them the partner leaving paid work. The public/private divide here is not a description of where care happens but a legal operation determining what counts as work. The 2022 Vietnam Time Use Survey (World Bank, 6,000 respondents) shows 45% of women performing household care against 24% of men; informal-sector women average 4.34 hours of unpaid care daily to men's 2.66 (ILO Vietnam, 2022). In 2019 \- the latest sex-disaggregated data \- only 16% of women 65+ received a pension against 27.3% of men; men's average pension exceeded women's by 19.8% (Ramírez López & Sengupta, 2024). Combining feminist legal methods (Bartlett, 1990\) with doctrinal analysis, the paper critiques the public/private divide (Olsen, 1983\) and draws on East Asian care insurance (Japan, Korea) and the German Pflegeversicherung. Drawing on Glenn's (2010) account of how law transforms care into status-based obligation, and Tronto's caution (Caring Democracy, 2013\) that market pricing distorts care's meaning, it develops recognised care labour: unpaid care given legal status and pension entitlements, not market services. Grounded in CEDAW General Recommendation No. 17 (1991) and the ILO Resolution concerning Decent Work and the Care Economy (2024), it proposes principles for legal reform across the fields that govern care \- social insurance, labour, family, and gender equality law \- framed as input for the Vietnam Women's Union, which holds legislative initiative on women's affairs. Recognition does not override the choice to prioritise care; it ensures the choice is made freely, not under economic pressure. Claiming that Vietnam cannot afford recognition is misframed: the state, market and households are already subsidised by women's unpaid care \- through lost pensions, eroded health and reduced labour participation. Recognising care labour does not abolish the public/private divide but refuses to let it determine legal standing \- the right to make claims against actors that have long benefited from invisible work.
International legal frameworks such as CEDAW, the Paris Agreement, and associated environmental conventions are among the most ambitious instruments feminist legal scholars and activists have mobilized to advance women's rights and environmental justice. Yet the record of implementation reveals a persistent and troubling pattern: states ratify, report, and formally comply, while the structural conditions that subordinate women to both economic precarity and environmental harm remain largely intact. This paper takes that gap as its central problem. Drawing on a comparative study of Vietnam and Norway, two states with markedly different political systems yet convergent formal commitments to gender equality and environmental governance, the paper interrogates what is gained, and what is lost, when feminist advocates work through international legal frameworks rather than around them. It applies a stance of sceptical pragmatism: neither dismissing legal mobilization as co-optation, nor accepting formal compliance as evidence of genuine transformation. The analysis is structured around three dimensions of legal failure. First, the paper examines how norm localization processes, drawing on Acharya's (2004) framework, systematically hollow out the transformative content of international norms as they are domesticated into national law. CEDAW's structural critique of patriarchal power, for instance, tends to be translated into technocratic gender mainstreaming mechanisms that leave underlying hierarchies undisturbed. Second, the paper interrogates climate governance frameworks through MacGregor's (2014) feminist ecological citizenship lens, showing how market-driven and individualist climate policy approaches — evident in both the Vietnamese green growth strategy and Norway's carbon trading mechanisms — effectively transfer responsibility from institutions to individuals, exempting economic actors from accountability while burdening women with adaptive labour. Third, drawing on Shove's (2010) analysis of social change theories, the paper critiques the neoliberal epistemologies embedded in gender-responsive climate frameworks, which tend to position women as resilient agents of behavioral change rather than as political claimants challenging the systemic causes of their disproportionate vulnerability. Empirically grounded in analysis of CEDAW shadow reports, national climate adaptation strategies, gender-responsive environmental assessments, and interviews with policymakers and civil society actors in both countries, the paper does not conclude that legal mobilization is futile. Rather, following Taylor (2000) conception of the state as a "contested terrain," it argues that legal frameworks are sites of ongoing struggle whose outcomes depend on the power of the actors deploying them and the coherence of the political coalitions behind them. The paper closes by proposing evaluative criteria for distinguishing legal reforms that genuinely shift power from those that perform transformation while preserving it — a contribution to both feminist legal theory and to the practical strategic choices facing women's rights advocates in climate-vulnerable communities.

A decision may cite gender standards and still reproduce patriarchal reasoning if it fails to examine the hidden assumptions underlying its legal and factual premises. Rather, it operates as a pedagogical and critical tool for identifying biases, stereotypes, silences, formalism, evidentiary deficits, and argumentative obstacles in legal texts. Family responsibilities discrimination in employment has increasingly emerged as a critical yet under-theorised issue in contemporary labour law. In Vietnam, labour legislation expressly recognises family responsibilities as a prohibited ground of discrimination. However, the legal meaning, normative consequences, and institutional implications of this recognition remain insufficiently developed. Existing legal protections continue to be predominantly organised around a maternity-protection paradigm, which plays an important role in safeguarding women’s reproductive and maternal functions but does not fully address the wider disadvantages experienced by workers who undertake caregiving responsibilities. This limitation is particularly significant in a labour market where care work remains unequally distributed. Women continue to bear a disproportionate burden of unpaid family care. Employment practices often reproduce gendered assumptions about productivity, availability, and commitment. Against this background, the article employs feminist legal theory as its principal analytical framework, combined with a doctrinal and normative analysis of Vietnamese labour law. This approach allows the article to examine not only the formal content of legal rules, but also the gendered assumptions embedded in their design and implementation. Feminist legal analysis is especially useful for interrogating the limits of a protective legal model that tends to associate care primarily with women’s maternity status, rather than recognising caregiving as a broader social and labour-market issue. At the same time, the article cautions against an uncritical embrace of formal gender neutrality. Although care-related rights should not be confined to women, formally neutral rules may fail to remedy substantive inequality if they overlook the unequal distribution of care. The article pursues three main objectives. First, it clarifies the feminist legal significance of family responsibilities discrimination in employment and explains why this form of discrimination should be understood as a structural barrier to equal labour-market participation. Secondly, it evaluates the limitations of the existing maternity-centred framework under Vietnamese labour law, particularly its tendency to protect women as mothers while leaving broader caregiving responsibilities insufficiently addressed. Thirdly, it proposes a shared-care approach to labour regulation, under which care-related rights and responsibilities are allocated more equitably among workers, employers, families, and the State. The article argues that Vietnamese labour law should move beyond a narrow maternity-protection model toward a substantive equality framework that combines gender-neutral access to care-related entitlements with targeted attention to women’s unequal care burdens. In doing so, it contributes to feminist labour law scholarship and offers a normative basis for reforming Vietnamese labour law in response to changing patterns of work, family, and care.
In India, FemTech applications designed for tracking sexual and reproductive health are put forth with a narrative of tools for empowerment, and have seen widespread adoption and user turnout. The fundamental question to ask is whether these applications promote gender justice or serve as a tool for data extraction and surveillance. Specifically, the DPDP Act’s reliance on a notice-and-consent mechanism fails to account for dark patterns and digital literacy that push users into exploitative data-sharing. From an intersectional perspective, the algorithmic biases in FemTech applications also play a part in the lives of marginalised women, and manifest in data invisibility, inaccurate predictions, and exclusion. Using a blended approach of socio-legal and feminist ethics, the authors will explore the disproportionate nature of these harms. This methodology serves as a form of feminist law-making by asking the question to expose how neutral data and privacy regulation fails to account for the lived realities of Indian women. This paper will examine the advantages that FemTech applications provide in overcoming social obstacles with empowerment, and will also critically review the subsequent harms to privacy and social surveillance, as a uniquely Indian risk. Further, it will illustrate the pitfalls in the DPDP Act that concern the protection of sensitive personal data by a data fiduciary. Ultimately, this paper will argue that positive legislative action is essential to achieving substantive gender justice through the imposition of a higher fiduciary duty on FemTech and digital healthcare providers, and it is proposed that these companies should be classified as data fiduciaries under privacy and data law in order to focus on the protection of the users, even where this may conflict with profit motives.

By shifting from carbon to care, the paper develops a feminist approach to climate adaptation governance that treats relational survival and everyday care practices not as private matters, but as central objects of legal protection and institutional design. This paper does not simply ask how women can be included in climate adaptation; it asks how adaptation governance must be rewritten once care is treated as climate infrastructure.

India's Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956 (ITPA) positions itself as protective legislation for marginalised women in prostitution. This paper argues that this positioning conceals a structural paradox: ITPA's enforcement architecture most systematically excludes the women it most explicitly claims to protect. The mechanism of exclusion is not administrative; it is doctrinal, rooted in the statute's foundational failure to account for caste as an axis of legal vulnerability irreducible to gender or class. Drawing on Uma Chakravarti's Brahmanical Patriarchy framework, this paper examines four hereditary sex work communities: the Devadasi, Bedia, Nat, and Kanjar. For women in these communities, entry into sex work is not a constrained individual choice; it is an intergenerational assignment inscribed in caste identity and hereditary occupational obligation. NHRC ethnographic documentation and Kotiswaran's fieldwork in Sonagachi and G.B. Road demonstrate that caste determines not merely the fact of entry but a worker's position within the industry's internal hierarchy. The premise of voluntary choice that underlies ITPA's rescue apparatus and proposed foreign transplants, namely the Dutch licensing model and New Zealand's decriminalisation framework, is, as Sen's capabilities framework confirms, a category error at the level of the legal subject. The paper advances three arguments. First, deploying Nils Christie's ideal victim construct, it demonstrates that ITPA produces a Victim-Offender Paradox: the statute's criminalising provisions (Sec. 3, 4, 5, 7, and 8\) target the provider rather than the exploiter, and caste-affected sex workers, who fail every criterion of Christie's ideal victim, are most comprehensively excluded from victim recognition while remaining fully exposed to criminal liability. Second, building on Crenshaw's intersectionality framework and Angela Harris's critique of gender essentialism, the paper demonstrates that MacKinnon-influenced radical feminism reproduces caste-based exclusion structurally: universalising the category of "woman under patriarchy" without interrogating caste as a prior axis of subordination generates the same erasures as the patriarchal legal order it critiques. The paper's contribution is to demonstrate this critique's doctrinal consequences provision-by-provision in ITPA's statutory design, showing how gender essentialism produces identifiable structural harm to its most marginalised subjects. Third, applying Cooper's prefigurative methodology, the paper proposes what a community-grounded re-drafting would require: entry as a distinct protective category; and constitutional anchoring in Article 21 and Ambedkar's constitutional morality. The paper's intervention is doctrinal, intersectional, and prefigurative: not a critique of the law from outside, but a feminist legislative draft built from inside the communities the law has long claimed to protect, and has systematically erased.
Centering Lived Experiences: Feminist Law-Making, Disability, and Inclusive SRHR Feminist law-making provides a critical way of understanding and transforming law by reshaping how laws are written, interpreted, and applied to challenge patriarchal assumptions embedded in legal systems. Although many laws appear gender-neutral, they often reflect male-centered norms and fail to capture the lived realities of women and girls with disabilities. This gap is particularly visible in sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR), where exclusion is often subtle yet deeply felt, shaped by the intersection of gender and disability. International and regional frameworks such as the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW), the Protocol to the African Charter on the Rights of Women in Africa, and the African Charter on Human and Peoples’ Rights provide important protections. However, these frameworks frequently treat women as a single, uniform group, limiting attention to intersectionality. As a result, they overlook how disability, gender, class, and social conditions interact to create layered forms of disadvantage. Consequently, women and girls with disabilities continue to face barriers in accessing SRHR services, experience limited autonomy over their bodies, receive weak protection in private spaces, and are often excluded from law- and policy-making processes. This paper applies feminist jurisprudence through feminist law-writing as its method. It begins by “asking the woman question” to reveal how laws that appear neutral can still disadvantage women and girls with disabilities. An intersectional approach is then used to examine how overlapping identities shape inequality. Feminist practical reasoning connects legal analysis to lived realities, while consciousness-raising uses lived experiences to challenge dominant legal assumptions. The paper also critiques the public/private divide, showing how SRHR and bodily autonomy are often treated as private matters and therefore insufficiently addressed. The findings show that formal equality—treating everyone the same—fails to achieve fairness in practice. In contrast, substantive equality, which accounts for different realities and starting points, offers a more effective approach. When intersectionality is ignored, laws continue to exclude women and girls with disabilities and undermine their agency. Feminist law-writing therefore provides an important tool for rethinking legal language and systems so that lived experience becomes central in law-making. The paper recommends inclusive legal reform that actively involves women and girls with disabilities. This includes ensuring accessible SRHR consultations, creating inclusive legal systems, improving accountability through disaggregated data, and recognizing lived experience as a form of legal knowledge essential for advancing bodily autonomy and meaningful inclusion.

This paper will present findings from our investigation of recent institutional law reform processes. Our paper will present key criteria for listening with a feminist ear in law reform. These criteria will underpin the development of a model for listening with a feminist ear in Phase 2 of our project, in which we will engage with prefigurative methodologies to workshop whether and how listening with a feminist ear can lead to transformative change through feminist lawmaking.

Under current Chinese law, the only legally recognised intimate relationship is heterosexual marriage registered under the Civil Code of PRC. However, some homosexual couples conduct special imitation aiming to obtain the right and obligation regime as similar to that of heterosexual couples as possible. The recent legal interpretation of cohabitation property permits cohabitants to arrange their property by agreement, which does not explicitly exclude homosexual couples. In practice, some notary offices notarise homosexual cohabitation property agreements, and a very small number of courts have also held that cohabitation can be interpreted as including same-sex relationships. Through signing a series of agreements, cohabitants can secure rights of inheritance from their cohabitants and other protections. Against this background, this paper asks whether the emerging contractualisation practices in China constitute queer inclusion, or instead reproduce heteronormative governance through conditional accommodation. Methodologically, it combines doctrinal analysis of Chinese private law and interpretations with a close reading of cases and notarial practices. It adopts a queer-feminist critique perspective and argues that these contractual arrangements erase queer specificity by implicitly framing queer intimacy as a matter of contractual autonomy and further reinforcing heterosexual marriage as the normative benchmark. Consequently, this paper holds that such imitation does not queer Chinese private law but domesticates homosexual relationships within existing patriarchal and heteronormative structures. Moving beyond critique, this paper adopts a prefigurative feminist law-making approach to explore transformative possibilities. It is based on the well-documented significance of “chosen families” and diverse kinship networks in queer communities, and recognises that not all individuals seek the same marriage-like legal recognition. Hence, the paper emphasises the need to provide legal choices rather than a single heteronormative template. On the basis of existing practices, the paper presents a DIY law-making experiment that imagines alternative “care agreements” and relevant supportive legislations that transcend heterosexual marriage. It also further explores the potential of gradually reshaping Chinese private law norms in ways that better value lived realities and social justice norms. This paper contributes to feminist law-making discourse by bringing the underexplored Chinese jurisdiction into global conversation. It demonstrates how heteronormative assumptions operate not only through exclusion but through conditional inclusion, and then illustrates pathways for proactive, prefigurative engagement. Ultimately, the paper advocates a shift from heterosexual marriage toward inclusive legal norms that can properly accommodate the diverse intimate relationships in contemporary China.
Unpaid care work remains one of the most essential yet under-recognized forms of labor in contemporary society. In Vietnam, care for children, older persons, persons with disabilities, and dependent family members is often treated as a moral responsibility within the household rather than as work that carries social and economic value. This paper examines unpaid care work as a central issue for feminist law-making in Vietnam. Productive labor in the market is recognized, regulated, and protected, while reproductive labor within the family is often naturalized and left to private responsibility. This standpoint established a relational, materialist epistemology that sees Dalit women's lived experience not as an additive identity but as a critical vantage point from which patriarchy, caste and class are legible together. At the same time, the law contains specific protective provisions designed to safeguard women's right to motherhood under particular circumstances. Nevertheless, the effective protection depends not only on the existence of legal provisions but also on their clarity, coherence, and consistency. Moreover, substantive gender equality can be achieved only when judicial decisions are fully respected and effectively enforced by the parties concerned. Against this background, this study examines the current Vietnamese legal framework to identify existing legal shortcomings and proposes recommendations for legal reform, further evaluating practical implementation through selected case studies.
Although the 2019 Labour Code of Vietnam establishes a number of specific protections for women nursing children under twelve months of age, it does not recognise a statutory right to remote work for this group. This legislative gap has become increasingly significant against the backdrop of Vietnam's national digital transformation agenda, as set out in Decision No. 749/QD-TTg of 2020, and the rapid development of broadband infrastructure, digital working platforms, and technology-driven human resource management practices. Drawing on doctrinal analysis of existing Vietnamese labour law and a comparative study of legislative trends across more than twenty jurisdictions — spanning common law systems (the United Kingdom, Australia, Canada, and New Zealand), European civil law systems (Portugal, the Netherlands, France, Spain, Sweden, Germany, and Italy), Asian systems (Japan, the Republic of Korea, and India), and Latin American systems (Chile and Argentina) — this article identifies a clear international shift away from purely leave-based maternity protection towards rights-based flexible working arrangements. The comparative evidence suggests that a request-based model, under which employers bear the burden of justifying any refusal in writing, represents the most effective balance between worker protection and employer autonomy. On this basis, the article argues that codifying a right to remote work for the group in question is both legally justified and practically feasible, and proposes a new statutory provision to be inserted adjacent to Article 137 of the Labour Code, operating on a presumption of approval and accompanied by concrete enforcement mechanisms and sanctions.
In 1995, the Supreme Court of Canada’s ruling in R v O’Connor, concerning a case of sexual violence, sparked significant debate over the right to privacy and the complainant’s obligation to disclose evidence to protect their legal rights and interests. At its core, R v O’Connor is a controversial decision because the complainant’s right to privacy and the duty to disclose evidence in a sexual assault case are closely tied to the broader issue of balancing the legal rights and interests of both complainants and defendants. Specifically, following fundamental principles of due process, the decision in O’Connor allowed for third-party access to the victim’s medical records. However, government data from 2019 indicate that 95% of sexual violence victims in Canada are women. The disclosure of victims’ medical records or other sensitive evidence, often involving women, can have serious consequences on their lives, particularly given the gendered burdens and societal expectations that women disproportionately bear. Following O’Connor, the decision has been cited in numerous subsequent cases, most notably in R v Mills (1999), along with the enactment of Bill C-46, which amended the Criminal Code to prioritize the complainant’s privacy rights over the accused’s right to full disclosure. This legislative and judicial shift has raised concerns over whether such prioritization might undermine the foundational principles of a fair trial. This paper aims to examine whether R v O’Connor can be reinterpreted through the lens of feminist legal theory, and if so, how the tension between a victim’s right to privacy and the duty to disclose relevant evidence should be addressed and balanced.
This paper evaluates the intersection of constitutional jurisprudence and workplace protections in Malaysia by applying the methodology of “feminist law-making” to landmark judicial decisions. The author argues that despite the legislative progress of the Anti-Sexual Harassment Act 2022, Malaysian courts remain constrained by a doctrinal approach that treats sexual harassment primarily as a private contractual breach or a disciplinary misconduct. This paper posits that such “neutral” adjudication fails to align with the constitutional values of human dignity. Utilising the “feminist judgment” framework (Hunter, 2010\) as a supplementary analytical tool, the paper performs a judicial reconstruction of a selected precedent, specifically targeting the “contract test” in constructive dismissal cases. By “asking the woman question”, the author demonstrates that the traditionally “objective” reasonable person standard is constitutionally inadequate for assessing hostile work environments. Instead, the paper proposes a practical reasoning that centres the victim’s perspective as a necessary requirement for epistemic justice. The core of this paper is a shift from private-law remedies to public-law guarantees. The author “re-imagines” the case through a prismatic interpretation of Article 5 (Right to Life) and Article 8 (Equality) of the Federal Constitution. By reframing sexual harassment as a constitutional injury, the paper provides a prefigurative model (Enright et al., 2025\) for how mainstream constitutional tools can be reconfigured to protect the “right to livelihood”, which is derived from the “right to life”.
This paper examines the compounding vulnerabilities of tribal women in India, shaped by the intersection of gender, Adivasi (indigenous) identity, landlessness, and political labelling. Despite the evolution of feminist legal theory, the specific lived realities of tribal women remain largely absent from mainstream jurisprudence, a gap originating from their exclusion during the drafting of the Constitution and persisting through modern legislative processes. The research critically analyses the “positionality” of tribal women, arguing that existing gender-responsive laws especially Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 previously known as Indian Penal Code, 1860 and the Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe (Prevention of Atrocity Act), 1989, while perhaps adequate for urban, upper-caste, or middle-class perspectives, fail to encapsulate the complexities of tribal women’s lives. By documenting specific cases of sexual assault, the paper highlights a significant judicial failure, the inability of the courts to interpret the factual matrix through a tribal woman’s standpoint. To provide a broader context, the study draws parallels with global discourses on intersectional gendered violence against indigenous women in other jurisdictions, evaluating the impact of their respective legal reforms and judicial interventions. The paper concludes by proposing a framework for legal reform that centres the voices of tribal women working at the grassroots. It advocates for a shift in feminist law-making that moves beyond symbolic representation toward a substantive inclusion of intersectional realities.
Social scoring is a sophisticated data retrieval, statistical, and inference system that primarily uses personal data about individuals' behavior on social media to foresee and predict their behavior. Social scoring has become a major concern regarding fairness, social order, and accessibility in society. This system amplifies gender inequality, particularly in labor and finance, through algorithms designed to target social discrimination based on gender biases. Firstly, biases about competence are based on assumptions about future events such as pregnancy, childbirth, and maternity leave. Secondly, biases about behavior are based on preconceived notions such as divorcées, single motherhood, and young women, thereby highlighting a "higher risk level" that significantly restricts access for these affected women. Using comparative legal analysis across the EU and Vietnam, the authors argue that existing legal frameworks are insufficient to address these gendered harms and that targeted legislative intervention is necessary. By examining regulatory approaches in the EU, the authors identify gaps and effective mechanisms in anti-discrimination and algorithmic accountability law, and propose concrete recommendations for law-making in Vietnam focusing on how legislators can design legal norms that incorporate the lived experience of affected women and build enforceable standards of gender-equality into the governance of scoring systems.
In Vietnam, surrogacy is legally recognized but only under stringent conditions and solely for humanitarian purposes. The 2014 Law on Marriage and Family and its guiding documents strictly regulate surrogacy to ensure it serves altruistic intentions and explicitly prohibits any form of commercial surrogacy arrangements. This is for protecting the rights and welfare of all parties involved, particularly surrogates, by preventing exploitation through commercial transactions. This article aims to provide a critical analysis of the existing legal provisions on commercial surrogacy in Vietnam. Then, through this examination, the article highlights the complexities and challenges inherent in enforcing these laws and protecting surrogate mothers’ rights in practice. Special attention is given to the legal and social consequences faced by the surrogates engaged in commercial surrogacy, especially in the case of dealing with the consequences of surrogacy after being criminally or administratively prosecuted, shedding light on the Vietnamese legal system’s broader perspective and policy approach toward such ethically sensitive and legally contentious issues. By delving into these aspects, we hope to propose some recommendations to widen and protect the rights of surrogates in Vietnam.
The shift toward remote work reveals that current laws defining the “workplace” are no longer effective since traditional labor statutes usually interpret this concept as a specific geographical location or a physical space where employees perform tasks under an employment contract. Consequently, when sexual harassment occurs in decentralized environments, workers find themselves in a legal gap without protection. To address, this research utilizes feminist legal theory to examine how the traditional “public/private” divide harms remote workers by categorizing remote working spaces as "private" areas where labor laws do not apply, which systematically leaves individuals vulnerable. Accordingly, the study argues that instead of a fixed physical location, the workplace should be understood as a “relational continuum”. Building upon this concept, this paper further proposes a model for identifying workplace and defining employer liability for sexual harassment in modern labor contexts based on feminist and comparative legal approach. By doing so, this study offers a doctrinal foundation for legislative reforms that ensure safety and accountability remain effective regardless of where the labor is performed, thereby protecting gender-vulnerable workers in the modern age.
