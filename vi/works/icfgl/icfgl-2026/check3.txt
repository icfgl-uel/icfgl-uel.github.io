4327: <p class="custom-details-content">In the late 1990s, Sharmila Rege argued for a Dalit feminist standpoint. This standpoint established a relational, materialist epistemology that sees Dalit women's lived experience not as an additive identity but as a critical vantage point from which patriarchy, caste and class are legible together. Rege argued that the question was not just who speaks, but from where knowledge is produced and what political-epistemic labour it does. Rege drew it on the Marxist standpoint tradition and reworked it through Phule-Ambedkarite thought. This paper suggests that Rege's standpoint theory is a methodological tool that Indian feminist legal scholarship has not yet fully processed. It also has significant ramifications for the global conversation about intersectional jurisprudence. The paper places Rege's intervention in the context of the Dalit-Bahujan feminist tradition, from Savitribai Phule and Tarabai Shinde in the nineteenth century to Ruth Manorama, Cynthia Stephen and the present-day scholar-activists like Christina Dhanaraj and Asha Kowtal. It demonstrates how Indian sexual violence and anti-discrimination law have failed Rege's standpoint test. Indian feminist legal reform, from the anti-rape amendments of 1983 to the post-Nirbhaya Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013, has largely focused on a caste-unmarked complainant. The caste-based sexual violence has been quarantined in the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989\. The paper illustrates this by examining the legislative debates and judicial reasoning in the Bhanwari Devi case (1992) and the Hathras case (2020). It indicates how the procedural and evidentiary structure of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act (POSH), 2013, and the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act (PoA), 1989 reproduces exactly the "difference-as-fragmentation" that Rege criticized: caste and gender are adjudicated in parallel, never as compound harm. Conviction patterns, evidentiary standards around "consent" and "caste knowledge" and the institutional separation of atrocity investigation from sexual violence investigation all serve to render the Dalit-Bahujan complainant's testimony legally illegible. The paper concludes with a suggestion as to what a Regean feminist legal method would entail in practice. The paper argues that Rege's standpoint theory is not a regional Indian phenomenon but a valuable, exportable contribution to the global feminist jurisprudence on intersectionality, inclusion and the law's relationship with the marginalised. Keywords: Sharmila Rege, Dalit feminist standpoint, difference, intersectional jurisprudence, Indian feminist legal reform.</p>
4335: <p class="custom-details-content">This paper examines the compounding vulnerabilities of tribal women in India, shaped by the intersection of gender, Adivasi (indigenous) identity, landlessness, and political labelling. Despite the evolution of feminist legal theory, the specific lived realities of tribal women remain largely absent from mainstream jurisprudence, a gap originating from their exclusion during the drafting of the Constitution and persisting through modern legislative processes. The research critically analyses the “positionality” of tribal women, arguing that existing gender-responsive laws especially Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 previously known as Indian Penal Code, 1860 and the Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe (Prevention of Atrocity Act), 1989, while perhaps adequate for urban, upper-caste, or middle-class perspectives, fail to encapsulate the complexities of tribal women’s lives. By documenting specific cases of sexual assault, the paper highlights a significant judicial failure, the inability of the courts to interpret the factual matrix through a tribal woman’s standpoint. To provide a broader context, the study draws parallels with global discourses on intersectional gendered violence against indigenous women in other jurisdictions, evaluating the impact of their respective legal reforms and judicial interventions. The paper concludes by proposing a framework for legal reform that centres the voices of tribal women working at the grassroots. It advocates for a shift in feminist law-making that moves beyond symbolic representation toward a substantive inclusion of intersectional realities.</p>

4505: <p class="custom-details-content">Feminist law-making must begin before the legal text is drafted: in the institutional conditions of legislative deliberation. Through an empirical analysis of Polish reproductive law debates, we show how women's voices may be formally present yet deprived of law-making agency. We therefore propose a Feminist Legislative Deliberation Project, oriented toward reconstructing not only what feminist law should say, but also how feminist law-making should listen. Inspired by the Feminist Legislation Project, we argue that, in the Polish context, a prior analytical step is necessary: the reconstruction of legislative deliberation as the space in which women's voices are either transformed into law-making agency or neutralized before they can shape legal text. This is especially important in light of the post-1989 transformation, the political authority of the Catholic Church, anti-communist moral language, and the weak institutionalization of feminist expertise. The empirical basis of the analysis is a corpus of transcripts from sessions of the Polish Sejm, parliamentary committees, and plenary sittings concerning reproductive law between 1992 and 2024, with particular attention to debates on the Act on Family Planning, Protection of the Human Foetus, and Conditions of Permissibility of Abortion. Drawing on Law and Emotions, feminist legal critique, and Dorothy Stetson's distinction between visible and invisible impact, we propose the category of elusive defeat: a situation in which women's voices are present in legislative space but systematically stripped of agency. We identify three interrelated mechanisms: the emotionalization of women's speech, procedural marginalization, and delegitimization through expertise. These mechanisms are not incidental, but form part of a legislative architecture that reproduces gendered norms of rationality and determines which voices, arguments, and experiences can shape law. On this basis, we formulate the assumptions of a Feminist Legislative Deliberation Project for Poland. We understand the rewriting of parliamentary debate as a reconstruction of the conditions of deliberation on the basis of selected fragments from Sejm and committee debates. This requires rewriting the frame of reproductive law debates, the rules of listening, the status of experience, the figure of the expert, and procedural interventions such as interruptions or withdrawals of the floor. Feminist deliberation would begin not from the question of exceptions to foetal protection, but from the state's obligations toward pregnant persons, their health, autonomy, safety, and equality. It would treat experience as legislative knowledge and require institutions to respond to women's voices rather than merely record their formal presence.</p>
