Giới thiệu hội thảo

ICFGL 2026 là mốc quan trọng trong hợp tác giữa UEL và Rosa-Luxemburg-Stiftung; đóng vai trò cầu nối giữa giai đoạn nền tảng của dự án và định hướng tương lai, đồng thời chính thức mở giai đoạn chiến lược 2027–2030.

Chúng tôi mời các học giả hàng đầu để thúc đẩy chuyển dịch phương pháp luật nữ quyền: từ diễn giải tư pháp sang soạn thảo lập pháp. Kể từ 2021, dự án đã đưa Lý thuyết pháp luật Nữ quyền (FLT) vào cộng đồng học thuật Việt Nam qua dòng «Bản án nữ quyền» — viết lại quá khứ để điều chỉnh thiên kiến lịch sử.

Trong lộ trình 2027–2030, dự án hướng tới vị trí «thượng nguồn» hơn trong quy trình pháp lý, chuyển trọng tâm sang «Lập pháp nữ quyền» — tức phác thảo tương lai. ICFGL 2026 là điểm xoay trí thức, quy tụ chuyên gia làm rõ mối liên hệ thiết yếu giữa cách pháp luật bị xét xử và cách pháp luật được viết ra.

From Ideas to Statutes: French Feminist Thought and the Reshaping of Vietnamese Law in the Colonial Era

Mr Le Ngoc Phu, Ms. Nguyen Thi Hoai
University of Law, Hue University Vietnam (Vietnam)

Đọc Tóm tắt

During the French colonial period in Vietnam (1858–1945), the legal status of women was shaped by an uneasy encounter between Confucian customary law, the feudal law of the Nguyễn dynasty, and the imported French civil-law tradition. This article analyses the transmission of French feminist thought into colonial Indochina, a process which gradually led to legal changes concerning women's rights. The article adopts an interdisciplinary approach combining three complementary layers of analysis. First, legal-historical analysis is employed to systematically examine the norms relating to women's rights in the key legal texts promulgated under colonial rule, together with the decrees and ordinances issued by the Governor-General of Indochina. Second, discourse analysis is applied to the press materials and the Vietnamese feminist movement — Nữ giới chung (1918), Phụ nữ tân văn (1929–1934), and Đàn bà mới (1934–1936) — in order to reconstruct how French feminist thought was received, translated, and transmitted within the colonial public sphere. Third, the comparative method is applied to juxtapose metropolitan and colonial law, thereby identifying both continuity and selectivity in the process of intellectual transmission and legal codification. On this basis, the article argues that French feminist thought produced not only visible statutory reforms but also a deeper "inconspicuous impact" by inscribing the very idea of women's rights into Vietnamese legal-political discourse. By recasting the colonial legal system as both an instrument of domination and an unintended vehicle of feminist legal modernization, the article contributes a non-Western case study to the feminist legal history from the 19th to 20th century. Keywords: Feminist thought, French, women's right, transformation, colonial law, inconspicuous impact .

Comparative Feminist Legal History of Women's 'Subtle Impact' in Vietnam and India: Invisible Reformers

Ms. Priyanka Gupta
School of Law, IILM University Greater Noida (India)

Đọc Tóm tắt

This paper examines India and Vietnam through the perspective of feminist legal historiography and the idea of “inconspicuous impact,” focusing on the often-overlooked ways women have shaped legal and social change. Conventional legal scenario tends to emphasize formal institutions, prominent male figures, and major legislative developments, while ignoring the quieter yet significant contributions by women in everyday life. Using a comparative socio-legal approach, the study explores how women in India and Vietnam contributed to legal transformation through everyday acts of resistance, participation in community leadership, family negotiations, local activism, and informal justice mechanisms. The paper argues that these less visible forms of participation are essential to understanding feminist legal history and the broader development of legal culture in India and Vietnam. feminist legal history should not be limited to official laws, records, or state archives alone. In the Indian context, women have played a significant role in shaping legal awareness related to inheritance rights, anti-dowry campaigns, and community-based justice initiatives. These efforts often operated outside formal legal institutions, Similarly, in Vietnam, women contributed to legal and social transformation through their involvement in agrarian reforms, socialist reconstruction, and local governance at the community level. Although these contributions were not always formally recognized, still they played an important role in shaping everyday understandings of justice, equality, and social responsibility. By comparing India and Vietnam, the paper demonstrates that despite their different political and historical trajectories India’s postcolonial constitutional framework and Vietnam’s socialist legal system women in both country exercised influence in subtle and often unacknowledged ways. In doing so, it contributes to feminist scholarship that aims to reconstruct legal history through marginalized voices, informal practices, and everyday acts of resistance that quietly shaped the legal and social development of both the countries. Keywords: Feminist, Socio-legal approach, Legal Awareness, Socialist Reconstruction, Invisible Reformers

From Imperial Compassion to Feminist Law-Making: Humanitarian Legal Treatment of Women Offenders in Vietnam

Mr Nhat Khanh Nguyen
University of Economics and Law, VNU-HCM; University of Law, VNU-Hanoi Vietnam (Vietnam)

Đọc Tóm tắt

This paper examines the humanitarian legal treatment of women who violate the law in Vietnam, covering both administrative violations and criminal offences, as a historical trajectory from imperial compassion to contemporary feminist law-making. Rather than understanding leniency or protection for women merely as benevolent exceptions, the paper asks how these legal practices have reflected, negotiated, and sometimes reinforced gendered assumptions about women’s bodies, motherhood, caregiving responsibilities, morality, and social vulnerability. The study begins with imperial Vietnamese legal traditions, where women who breached legal or moral norms could receive mitigated treatment in certain circumstances, especially in relation to pregnancy, motherhood, family duties, age, or perceived feminine vulnerability. These practices were often framed through Confucian moral order and paternalistic compassion rather than women’s autonomy or substantive equality. The paper then traces how similar humanitarian concerns continue in modern Vietnamese law, not only in criminal law and criminal procedure, but also in the law on handling administrative violations. Contemporary legal provisions concerning pregnant women, mothers of young children, elderly women, and women in custodial or correctional settings reveal an enduring legal concern with reducing hardship for particular categories of female violators. However, this humanitarian approach raises a central feminist question: does the law genuinely transform women’s unequal position, or does it preserve patriarchal assumptions by valuing women mainly through reproductive, maternal, and caregiving roles? By placing Vietnamese legal history in dialogue with feminist law-making, this paper argues that humanitarian treatment should be reimagined not as exceptional mercy granted to “deserving” women, but as a foundation for reconstructing legal responses to violations around substantive equality, lived experience, and social vulnerability. A feminist law-making approach would move beyond gender-neutral sanctioning and beyond paternalistic protection. It would require lawmakers to consider how poverty, domestic violence, unpaid care burdens, migration, informal labour, social stigma, and unequal access to legal support shape women’s pathways into administrative and criminal violations, as well as their experiences within sanctioning systems. The paper shows that humanitarian legal provisions for women who violate the law in Vietnam embody both inherent limitations and transformative possibilities. Although such provisions may reinforce patriarchal assumptions regarding women’s social roles, they also offer a basis for reconstructing legal responsibility, systems of sanctioning, and understandings of justice through feminist principles centred on substantive equality and recognition of social vulnerability.

State, Nation, and Patriarchy: Gendered Inequality in the Evolution of Thai Nationality Law Affecting Thai Mothers and Wives

Assist.Prof.Dr. Kamonwan Yoowattana, Chonruitai Kaewrungrueng & Khamphiou Vilayphone
Faculty of Law, Suan Sunandha Rajabhat University (SSRU), Bangkok, Thailand Faculty of Law and Political Science, National University of Laos (NUOL), Laos PDR (Thailand)

Đọc Tóm tắt

This article, entitled “State, Nation, and Patriarchy: Gendered Inequality in the Evolution of Thai Nationality Law Affecting Thai Mothers and Wives,” examines how Thai nationality law has been shaped by state formation, nationalism, and patriarchal social structures. It explores how nationality law historically produced gendered inequalities affecting Thai women in their roles as mothers and wives. The study employs a qualitative socio-legal approach, drawing upon legal documents, legal history, principles of private international law relating to nationality and personal status, feminist legal theory, and the concept of gendered citizenship. The study finds that prior to the emergence of the modern nation-state, recognition of community membership and Thai identity was not exclusively dependent on paternal authority. Influenced by customary practices, both fathers and mothers could generally contribute to the recognition of a child’s belonging. However, with the development of the modern state and the influence of international legal frameworks during the colonial era, Thailand formalized nationality through centralized legislation and state control. Within this transformation, Thai nationality law increasingly privileged the status and rights of fathers and husbands over those of mothers and wives. Gendered inequalities were particularly evident in nationality transmission, acquisition of nationality through marriage, and naturalization policies. At various points in Thai legal history, Thai mothers faced greater restrictions than Thai fathers in passing nationality to their children, while foreign husbands of Thai women were treated less favorably than foreign wives of Thai men. These distinctions reflected patriarchal assumptions concerning family authority, citizenship, and national belonging, demonstrating how nationality law functioned not only as a legal framework for determining status but also as a mechanism for reproducing gender hierarchies. The article further traces reforms aimed at reducing these inequalities through successive amendments to Thai nationality law. It argues that these developments reflect Thailand’s efforts to align nationality law with international human rights standards, gender equality principles, and contemporary understandings of citizenship. The Thai experience offers an important case study for understanding the relationship between gender, state power, and nationality law in Southeast Asia and may contribute to broader discussions on women’s nationality rights and gender equality across the region.

Rethinking Women's Inheritance Rights in Vietnam: Law, Gender, and the Dynamics of Legal Change

Dr. Bach Thi Nha Nam & Ms. Le Nguyen Hong Nhung
University of Economics and Law, VNU-HCM Vietnam (Vietnam)

Đọc Tóm tắt

This article examines the transformation of women’s inheritance rights in Vietnam from the premodern period to the present, situating legal change within broader shifts in gender, kinship, and property relations. Drawing on foundational legal sources, including the Hong Duc Code (Quốc triều hình luật) and the Gia Long Code (Hoàng Việt luật lệ), it argues that premodern Vietnamese law neither excluded women entirely from inheritance nor recognised them as fully autonomous legal subjects. Rather, women’s entitlements were relational and contextually bounded within Confucian patriarchal structures that prioritised lineage continuity, moral obligation, and ancestral worship. Adopting a socio-legal and diachronic approach, the article traces how these historically embedded entitlements have been reconfigured under modern legal frameworks, particularly through the Civil Code 2015 and contemporary judicial practice. It shows that legal modernisation has not produced a uniform trajectory of gender equality, but has instead generated differentiated patterns in the recognition and realisation of women’s inheritance rights. These patterns are shaped through multiple mechanisms of legal change, including judicial interpretation, legal categorisation, and the continued influence of customary and moral norms. By conceptualising women’s inheritance rights as historically layered and socially mediated, the article challenges linear narratives of legal progress. It demonstrates that formal legal recognition does not necessarily dismantle gender hierarchy, but may reconfigure it in new forms. The study contributes to broader debates on gender, law, and legal modernity in Asia by offering an account of legal hybridity grounded in processes of differentiation, negotiation, and selective realisation.

From Private Harm to Public Justice: A Feminist Mixed-Methods Analysis of Procedural Injustice and Legal Disengagement

Jevilyn Madalang
Cordilleras University, Philippines

Đọc Tóm tắt

Violence against women intersects profoundly within the family, triggering self-perpetuating cycles of emotional and physical abuse that are often reinforced by the very legal systems designed to provide recourse. This study examines the phenomenon of systemic case attrition in violence against women (VAW) litigation within the specific socio-legal context of Baguio City, Philippines. Moving beyond traditional criminological frameworks that often attribute case withdrawal to victim non-cooperation, this research employs a feminist legal critique to frame attrition as a structural byproduct of the tension between formal law, familial intimacy, and gendered power relations. Utilizing an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design, the study first investigates the lived experiences of survivors navigating the judicial apparatus. Qualitative findings show that many women face financial, familial, and procedural challenges that affect their decision to continue with the formal justice process.These insights are subsequently validated through a quantitative analysis of 1,083 cases spanning a five-year period. The quantitative phase, utilizing multinomial logistic regression, identifies victim-offender relationship and victim age as the primary predictors of attrition. Findings suggest that victims abused by family members or older victims are statistically more likely to withdraw, highlighting how the "private" nature of domestic violence intersects with institutional design to discourage formal recourse. The findings uncover a "case buildup" crisis characterized by an over-reliance on testimonial evidence, reducing complex cycles of abuse to a "your story against mine" paradigm and chronic trial delays. The research further argues that the justice system inadvertently reproduces the public/private divide by relegating intimate violence to the domain of informal compromise,. Consequently, mechanisms such as "amicable settlements" and "provisional dismissals", are re-conceptualized not as neutral legal outcomes, but as forms of "shortcut justice" adopted by survivors to escape an insensitive, resource-depleted, and protracted system Ultimately, this paper posits that attrition is a manifestation of institutional design that fails to account for the unique vulnerabilities of women within the family unit. The study concludes with a proposed policy framework centered on survivor-centered support mechanisms. This model seeks to dismantle the architecture of attrition by prioritizing collaborative investigation and substantive protection, ensuring that the legal system serves as a genuine site of redress rather than a secondary site of victimization.

The Private is Political, But the Law is Not: Rethinking the Public/Private Divide in Vietnamese and Nepali Legal Frameworks Through Feminist Law-Making

Ms. Nisha Khatri
Kathmandu School of Law Nepal (Nepal)

Đọc Tóm tắt

The public/private dichotomy remains one of the most enduring structural barriers to gender justice in both Vietnam and Nepal. By confining domestic labour, intimate partner violence, and family relations to the "private" sphere, law has historically rendered these domains invisible, beyond the reach of rights, remedy, and redistribution. This paper argues that feminist law-making offers a transformative methodology to dismantle this divide and revalue the private sphere in both jurisdictions, drawing on a comparative feminist legal analysis grounded in the works of Simone de Beauvoir, Carol Pateman, Martha Fineman, Angela Davis, and bell hooks. Building on de Beauvoir's foundational insight that woman is constructed as the "Other"; relegated to immanence and domesticity while men occupy the transcendent public world, this paper interrogates how Vietnamese and Nepali legal systems have codified and reproduced this subordination. Carol Pateman's critique of the sexual contract reveals how the public/private split is not a neutral legal boundary but a gendered political construction that legitimises patriarchal authority within the home. Martha Fineman's vulnerability theory further destabilises the liberal legal subject, exposing how the law's refusal to recognise dependency and care work perpetuates the economic marginalisation of women in both contexts. Through analysis of Vietnamese family law, labour legislation, and domestic violence frameworks alongside Nepal's Domestic Violence (Crime and Punishment) Act 2009 and Civil Code provisions, this paper demonstrates how formal gender-neutral language masks substantive inequality. Angela Davis's intersectional critique foregrounds how class and ethnicity compound the private/public exclusion for ethnic minority women in Vietnam's Central Highlands and Nepal's Madhesi and Dalit communities. bell hooks's emphasis on the politics of domination and love as a site of resistance further enriches the analysis by centring lived experience as a legal resource. This paper proposes that feminist law-making through rewritten legislative drafts, prefigurative law reform, and feminist judgment methodology can reconceptualise domestic labour as economically cognisable work, reframe intimate violence as a structural rather than personal failure, and redesign family law to reflect equitable distribution of care. Rather than accommodating women within existing patriarchal frameworks, this paper envisions a feminist rewriting of the private sphere itself as a site of rights, dignity, and redistributive justice.

Gendering the Common Good: Toward a Gender-Responsive Common Good Constitutionalism in Intellectual Property Law

Dr. Chiou, Tzu-Yu
Hsuan Chuang University (Taiwan)

Đọc Tóm tắt

Despite decades of legal reform, women and non-binary artists remain systematically disadvantaged: earning less, receiving less recognition, and watching their works sell at significant discounts relative to male counterparts. Intellectual property law has not merely failed to remedy this disparity—it has actively contributed to it. This chapter argues that the persistence of gender bias in IP regimes reflects a deeper jurisprudential failure: the law's inability to see who its frameworks exclude. Adrian Vermeule's Common Good Constitutionalism (CGC) offers a promising corrective. By rejecting liberal neutrality and demanding that legal institutions actively pursue substantive justice, CGC provides the institutional vocabulary for confronting structural inequality. Yet CGC harbors its own blind spot: its conception of the "common good" inherits from natural law tradition a putatively universal legal subject that has historically excluded women and non-binary persons from its normative vision. This chapter proposes an internal correction to CGC through dialogue with Martha Fineman's vulnerability theory and Kimberlé Crenshaw's intersectionality framework. Fineman supplies what CGC lacks—an analytical mechanism for identifying how legal institutions produce and sustain gendered vulnerability. Crenshaw refines this further by revealing how gender intersects with race, class, and disability to compound exclusion at the margins. Drawing on Taiwan as the primary jurisdictional focus, this chapter examines two connected sites of IP-based gender exclusion. First, Taiwan's copyright attribution rules systematically render invisible the collective and craft-based creative labor historically performed by women—a structural erasure embedded in the law's conception of authorship. Second, the rise of AI-generated art platforms introduces a new layer of algorithmic bias that replicates and intensifies these pre-existing hierarchies. These are not treated as separate phenomena but as connected moments in a longer trajectory of institutionalized exclusion, one that a gender-blind CGC cannot adequately diagnose or remedy. Drawing these threads together, this chapter proposes Gender-Responsive Common Good Constitutionalism (GRCGC) as an alternative normative framework, built on three principles: vulnerability visibility, intersectional accountability, and institutional responsiveness. A common good that cannot see who it excludes is not common at all.

Recognizing Women's Economic Contributions in the Division of Marital Property upon Divorce: A Feminist Legal Analysis of a Vietnamese Court Judgment

Ms. Nguyen Thuy Linh & Mr. Tran Ngoc Hoang Quan
Ho Chi Minh City University of Law; Ho Chi Minh City People's Court Vietnam (Vietnam)

Đọc Tóm tắt

Economic capacity plays a significant role in shaping the gendered status of both men and women. However, in the context of socio-economic development, women have increasingly demonstrated their distinct capabilities, which warrant full and equitable recognition. In practice, women’s participation in the labor market and their economic contributions to the family have grown substantially. Alongside income-generating activities, women continue to assume primary responsibility for childcare and the organization of family life. Even in cases where women do not directly produce material wealth, their roles in domestic work and family care should be acknowledged as a form of indirect economic contribution. By maintaining a stable household environment, they enable other family members \- particularly their husbands \- to concentrate on and maximize their productive capacities in the public sphere, thereby indirectly contributing to the generation of shared material wealth. This perspective reflects a fair and reasonable approach, as, in the absence of such domestic contributions, the husband’s time resources and labor productivity would be significantly divided and diminished. Accordingly, the role of women within both the family and society is indispensable and should not be underestimated. The case selected by the authors for presentation and analysis concerns the recognition and evaluation of gender factors in judicial decision-making. Specifically, the first-instance judgment applied existing legal provisions on the division of marital property upon divorce in a manner that remained rigid and somewhat one-dimensional, as it failed to adequately consider gender-related aspects. These include the nature and extent of contributions to the marital property, as well as the gender-specific characteristics associated with women’s roles \- particularly their psychological dimensions and responsibilities as mothers and wives. This issue has not yet received comprehensive and thorough attention from competent authorities or from society at large. By employing feminist legal theory in conjunction with doctrinal legal analysis and synthesis methods, this article evaluates the court’s decision with respect to the key issues of the case, offering critical observations, assessments, and conclusions.

How Feminist is Vietnam's Inheritance Law? Revisiting the Public-Private Divide in Inheritance Disputes

Dr. Bùi Thị Bích Liên
FPT University Vietnam (Vietnam)

Đọc Tóm tắt

This paper investigates how feminist Vietnamese inheritance law is when patriarchal tradition continues to influence inheritance practices and judicial resolution of inheritance disputes. Drawing on socio-legal analysis and empirical findings, the paper argues that the state recognition of equal inheritance rights has not dismantled the gendered power structures embedded within family and kinship relations. Shaped by historical circumstances, the Vietnamese state adopted gender-neutral rules of succession to support its broader socio-economic goals. Official discourse often presents inheritance law as proof of socialist equality and women’s emancipation according to which daughters and sons are legally entitled to equal shares of inheritance. Yet inheritance disputes expose an ongoing gap between formal legal equality and customary norms grounded in Confucian patrilineal traditions. As a result, courts often accommodate these social norms when resolving inheritance disputes. The paper critiques the traditional separation between the “private” family sphere and the “public” domain of state law. It argues that Vietnamese inheritance law illustrates how gender inequality is reproduced through the legal system’s deference to supposedly private cultural norms. Rather than transforming patriarchal inheritance structures, courts often mediate between state law and customary expectations in ways that preserve male privilege. The paper further argues that the Vietnamese experience complicates liberal feminist assumptions that formal equality alone can produce substantive gender justice. The paper contributes to feminist jurisprudence by providing an empirical account of how inheritance disputes become sites of negotiation between law, kinship, and gendered social expectations. In addition, it highlights the limitations of reform in contexts where customs continue to possess normative authority. We suggest instead that institutional law reform must attend to the listening process beyond voice, and specifically to listening with a feminist ear, to form a foundation for feminist lawmaking. Our project investigates institutional law reform processes – such as law reform commissions, parliamentary inquiries and Royal Commissions – as both a facet of, and a precursor to, formal lawmaking, with a specific focus on violence against women. We explore how such processes elicit women’s voices, particularly the voices of victim-survivors and diverse women with lived experience, and ask whether and how law reform institutions adopting feminist-informed listening practices can lead to more effective feminist lawmaking. This paper will present findings from our investigation of recent institutional law reform processes. We have identified significant variation in approaches to eliciting women’s voices, including embedding lived experience through advisory committees, partnerships with advocacy organisations and engaging women with lived experience through written submissions, roundtables and public hearings. Our research reveals that although these design choices can increase the visibility of women’s voices in law reform, they do not guarantee that their voices will be listened to in ways that influence institutional methods and frameworks. Drawing on Sara Ahmed’s theorisation of the feminist ear, we argue that law reform institutions must attend to their own listening practices and conceptualise listening as response-ability before women’s voices can contribute meaningfully to transformative change. Law reform must develop strategies for listening that dismantle colonial and gendered hierarchies, that listen for silence and for what is not and cannot be said, and that are prepared to challenge the patriarchal assumptions that underpin the legal status quo. Our paper will present key criteria for listening with a feminist ear in law reform. These criteria will underpin the development of a model for listening with a feminist ear in Phase 2 of our project, in which we will engage with prefigurative methodologies to workshop whether and how listening with a feminist ear can lead to transformative change through feminist lawmaking.

Towards Feminist Legislative Deliberation. A case study from Poland

Dr Lidia M. Rodak & Dr Marlena Drapalska-Grochowicz
University of Silesia Poland (Poland)

Đọc Tóm tắt

Feminist law-making must begin before the legal text is drafted: in the institutional conditions of legislative deliberation. Through an empirical analysis of Polish reproductive law debates, we show how women's voices may be formally present yet deprived of law-making agency. We therefore propose a Feminist Legislative Deliberation Project, oriented toward reconstructing not only what feminist law should say, but also how feminist law-making should listen. Inspired by the Feminist Legislation Project, we argue that, in the Polish context, a prior analytical step is necessary: the reconstruction of legislative deliberation as the space in which women's voices are either transformed into law-making agency or neutralized before they can shape legal text. This is especially important in light of the post-1989 transformation, the political authority of the Catholic Church, anti-communist moral language, and the weak institutionalization of feminist expertise. The empirical basis of the analysis is a corpus of transcripts from sessions of the Polish Sejm, parliamentary committees, and plenary sittings concerning reproductive law between 1992 and 2024, with particular attention to debates on the Act on Family Planning, Protection of the Human Foetus, and Conditions of Permissibility of Abortion. Drawing on Law and Emotions, feminist legal critique, and Dorothy Stetson's distinction between visible and invisible impact, we propose the category of elusive defeat: a situation in which women's voices are present in legislative space but systematically stripped of agency. We identify three interrelated mechanisms: the emotionalization of women's speech, procedural marginalization, and delegitimization through expertise. These mechanisms are not incidental, but form part of a legislative architecture that reproduces gendered norms of rationality and determines which voices, arguments, and experiences can shape law. On this basis, we formulate the assumptions of a Feminist Legislative Deliberation Project for Poland. We understand the rewriting of parliamentary debate as a reconstruction of the conditions of deliberation on the basis of selected fragments from Sejm and committee debates. This requires rewriting the frame of reproductive law debates, the rules of listening, the status of experience, the figure of the expert, and procedural interventions such as interruptions or withdrawals of the floor. Feminist deliberation would begin not from the question of exceptions to foetal protection, but from the state's obligations toward pregnant persons, their health, autonomy, safety, and equality. It would treat experience as legislative knowledge and require institutions to respond to women's voices rather than merely record their formal presence.

Deferred Equality and Feminist Law-Making in India: Women’s Reservation, Parliamentary Exclusion, and Gender Diversity in the Higher Judiciary

Dr. Naresh Prajapati
School of Law, Bennett University India (India)

Đọc Tóm tắt

India’s constitutional promise of equality has repeatedly been carried forward through reformist language, yet women’s presence in the institutions that make, interpret, and enforce law remains limited. The Women’s Reservation Act, 2023, marked an important constitutional moment by promising one-third reservation for women in the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies.However, the continuing uncertainty around its implementation, including the recent failure of the proposed constitutional amendment, reveals a familiar pattern: equality is formally recognised, but institutionally deferred. At the same time, women remain seriously underrepresented in the higher judiciary. Official data shows that, as of 02.02.2026, the Supreme Court had only one-woman judge, while the High Courts together had 116 women judges out of 814 working judges . This paper examines the connected exclusion of women from parliamentary law-making and higher judicial decision-making in India. Drawing on feminist legal theory, feminist constitutionalism, and the distinction between descriptive and substantive representation, it asks whether constitutional democracy can claim transformative equality when women remain marginal in both the legislature and the constitutional courts. The study adopts a doctrinal and socio-legal methodology, analysing constitutional provisions, the Women’s Reservation Act, parliamentary developments, judicial appointment data, and feminist scholarship on law-making and judging. It also engages with the conference theme of feminist law-making and judgments by treating representation not merely as numerical inclusion, but as a condition for reshaping legal reasoning, institutional legitimacy, and democratic accountability. The paper finds that India’s current model produces a gap between constitutional symbolism and institutional practice. Women’s reservation without timely implementation weakens feminist law-making, while gender imbalance in the higher judiciary narrows the possibility of feminist judging. The paper argues that feminist constitutionalism requires synchronised reform across Parliament and the judiciary, so that women are not only subjects of law, but active authors and interpreters of constitutional justice. Dr. Shuxi YIN
Hefei University of Technology, China (China)

Đọc Tóm tắt

This paper examines the possibilities and limitations of feminist law-making in contemporary China through the lens of gender equality legislation, digital feminist activism, and the evolving relationship between state law and grassroots legal consciousness. While feminist jurisprudence has often portrayed law as a patriarchal institution that reproduces structural inequality, recent developments in China demonstrate a more complex interaction between feminist advocacy, authoritarian governance, and legal reform. The paper argues that Chinese feminist actors increasingly engage in a form of “pragmatic feminist law-making” that operates both within and beyond formal legal institutions, thereby contributing to the gradual transformation of legal discourse on gender, labor, family, and violence. The study focuses on three interrelated areas: the anti-domestic violence framework following the enactment of the Anti-Domestic Violence Law (2015), gender discrimination in employment and reproductive rights, and the rise of digital feminist mobilization surrounding sexual harassment and the \#MeToo movement in China. Rather than evaluating feminist legal advocacy solely by immediate legislative outcomes, this paper highlights the “inconspicuous impact” of feminist interventions in reshaping public legal consciousness, judicial interpretation, and administrative governance. In doing so, it engages directly with Theme 1 of the conference concerning overlooked feminist influence on legal development. At the same time, the paper addresses Theme 3 by critically assessing the limits of legal mobilization in a political environment where feminist activism faces institutional constraints and censorship. It argues that Chinese feminist actors adopt a form of “skeptical pragmatism”: they strategically utilize existing legal language, state policy discourse, and administrative complaint mechanisms while remaining aware of the patriarchal and political boundaries embedded in the legal system. This strategy allows feminists to negotiate partial reforms without fully endorsing the legitimacy of existing structures. Methodologically, the paper combines feminist legal theory with socio-legal analysis and draws upon legal texts, court cases, digital feminist campaigns, and policy debates in contemporary China. It further explores how feminist law-making can incorporate women’s lived experiences into legal discourse in contexts where formal democratic participation remains limited. Ultimately, the paper contributes to broader debates on feminist DIY law-making by demonstrating how feminist legal consciousness can emerge not only through formal legislative drafting, but also through everyday acts of interpretation, digital resistance, and grassroots legal engagement.

Slanting the Lines of International Legality: Feminist Failures and the Gender Politics of International Lawmaking

Dr Juliana Santos de Carvalho
University of Antwerp Belgium (Belgium)

Đọc Tóm tắt

Feminist critiques to international legality often point how feminist projects are frequently excluded from dominant lawmaking processes or relegated to legal forms considered secondary, irregular, or insufficiently binding—such as soft law instruments, expert principles, or ambiguous normative formulations. Yet, these critiques have also tended to preserve the very distinction they critique: a stable line between “law” and “non-law,” success and failure, legality and its absence. This book argues that feminist international lawmaking should instead be approached through the blurred, unstable, and contested spaces between these binaries. Drawing on queer and feminist theory—particularly Sara Ahmed’s queer phenomenology, Judith Butler’s performativity, and Jack Halberstam’s queer art of failure—this book proposes a shift from an either/or understanding of legality towards an and/or sensitivity attentive to the plural meanings legality acquires in practice. Rather than asking whether feminist projects in international law successfully become “law,” this book asks what new political and epistemological possibilities emerge when legality itself is treated as socially constructed, performative, and contested. The argument is developed through three emblematic case studies in feminist international lawmaking: the Women, Peace and Security agenda of the UN Security Council; the Yogyakarta Principles on Sexual Orientation and Gender Identity; and the debates surrounding the non-definition of “gender” in the draft Convention on Crimes Against Humanity. Across these examples, the paper identifies three conceptual figures for rethinking feminist international lawmaking: shadows, drag, and silence. “Shadows” capture legal projects that operate with powerful normative effects despite their uncertain formal status; “drag” illuminates how imperfect mimicry of legal forms exposes the performative character of international legality itself; and “silence” reveals how ambiguity and non-definition may function not as absence, but as strategic resistance against restrictive legal categorisations. Methodologically, this book combines feminist and queer theory with qualitative empirical research, including semi-structured interviews, discourse analysis, and the reconstruction of international lawmaking practices from the ground up. In doing so, it contributes to emerging conversations on feminist international lawmaking by challenging the assumption that feminist legal projects must conform to pre-existing legal scripts to count as successful. Instead, it argues that feminist engagements with international law often generate transformative possibilities precisely through their apparent incompleteness, ambiguity, and deviation from conventional legal forms.

Feminist Anti-Corruption Law-Making: Rewriting Integrity Frameworks Around Women's Lived Experiences in Euro-Arab Cooperation

Mr. Elias El Hayek
University of Sussex (GI-ACE Governance & Integrity Fellowship); Beirut Bar Association, Lebanon (Lebanon)

Đọc Tóm tắt

Corruption is often treated as a governance failure, a technical compliance problem, or a matter of institutional efficiency. This paper argues that it should also be understood as a feminist legal problem. Across Europe and the Arab region, corruption does not affect all people in the same way. Women, especially those in marginalized communities, encounter corruption through gendered forms of harm: sextortion, exclusion from political and administrative decision-making, unequal access to public services, workplace coercion, and the diversion of social resources on which they often depend. Yet anti-corruption laws and strategies frequently remain written in gender-neutral language that fails to name these harms or respond to women's lived realities. Using feminist law-making as a methodology, this paper asks how anti-corruption frameworks would look if they were drafted not only to punish bribery or abuse of office, but to confront the patriarchal and institutional conditions that make gendered corruption possible. The paper builds on Euro-Arab examples of women's leadership in anti-corruption and argues these experiences should inform the design of laws, institutions, and cooperation agendas. It proposes a feminist anti-corruption law-making agenda built around three layers: recognizing sextortion and gendered abuse of power as corruption-related harms; adapting whistleblower protection, access to information, public procurement, and audit systems to women's specific risks; and supporting women's leadership in oversight institutions through Euro-Arab cooperation. By moving beyond gender mainstreaming toward feminist law-writing, the paper offers a practical model for transforming anti-corruption law into a feminist accountability tool.
