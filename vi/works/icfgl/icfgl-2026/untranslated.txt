3865: <p class="custom-details-content">Across many jurisdictions, digital governance and AI-assisted dispute resolution are increasingly promoted as tools for improving access to justice in domestic violence cases. Yet feminist and socio-legal scholarship questions whether platformized justice systems genuinely enhance protection for survivors or instead reshape which forms of gendered violence become legally visible, evidentially legible, and institutionally actionable. This study explores the adjudication and characteristics of domestic violence divorce cases in China within the context of "smart courts" and digital judicial governance. Based on 328 divorce judgments from the China Online Judgments Database (2023), the study combines feminist socio-legal analysis and empirical methods to assess evidentiary practices, judicial recognition of domestic violence, and case outcomes. The results show that domestic violence is rarely formally recognized (3.89%), with most judgments either ignoring or dismissing the allegations. Victims often face severe evidentiary limitations; only 22.96% of victims submitted supporting evidence, primarily informal materials such as photos and chat logs, rather than formal documents. Even when domestic violence is recognized, it does not always lead to divorce, compensation, or strong protections. Instead, case outcomes are largely influenced by repeated litigation, procedural screening, and compromise-oriented child custody arrangements. According to the findings, the trend of digital judicial governance does not appear to have significantly impacted the traditional logic of family law adjudication. Instead, it may have reinforced existing stability-oriented practices, making the handling of domestic violence cases more procedurally efficient, but not substantively protective. The paper argues that digital governance may enhance institutional responsiveness, but such improvement is selective rather than uniform. Platformization can facilitate earlier reporting, faster referral, inter-agency coordination, and the accumulation of standardized evidence, thereby increasing the procedural visibility of certain cases within judicial systems. However, it may also accelerate diversion into mediation-oriented pathways, where domestic violence is reframed as family conflict rather than structural abuse, potentially narrowing access to formal remedies. More fundamentally, digital systems tend to privilege harms that are easily recorded and categorized—such as physical injuries, repeated police contact, or traceable digital communication—while marginalizing coercive control, psychological abuse, and economic domination, which are difficult to translate into standardized evidentiary forms. In this sense, digitalized governance expands procedural access without resolving deeper evidentiary and interpretive constraints in domestic violence adjudication. Overall, the value of digital governance in domestic violence adjudication should not be assessed solely through efficiency or coordination metrics. Instead, it must be evaluated in terms of how technological infrastructures reshape the legal intelligibility and epistemic visibility of gendered harm. While digital systems may expand procedural access, they risk reproducing structural blind spots in recognizing non-physical and coercive forms of domestic violence.</p>
3873: <p class="custom-details-content">This article examines liability for compensation for mental damages arising from infringements upon honor, dignity, and reputation under Vietnamese civil law from the perspective of feminist legal theory, with a view to clarifying the limitations of traditional legal approaches to gendered emotional harm. As a result, many forms of gender-based emotional harm have not been adequately recognized or properly assessed in the process of legal application. In addition, the determination of compensation in many cases remains largely symbolic and lacks humane, gender-sensitive evaluative criteria that adequately take into account the victim’s lived experience, thereby undermining the effectiveness of protecting personality rights, the intrinsic value of human dignity, and the justice-oriented function of non-contractual damages liability. From the perspective of feminist legal theory, the article, through a case study on violations of women’s honor, dignity, and personal integrity, proposes a restructuring of the approach to liability for mental damages. In particular, it advocates a victim-centered approach that places the victim’s lived experience at the core of legal assessment and acknowledges the impact of gender inequality in the formation of emotional harm. Furthermore, the article recommends refining the criteria for determining damages and compensation in a flexible and individualized manner capable of ensuring substantive justice. In doing so, the article contributes to the theoretical foundation for the study of non-contractual liability from the perspective of gender justice, while also offering several recommendations for the further development of Vietnamese civil law in the context of strengthening the protection of human rights and personality rights today.</p>

4165: <p class="custom-details-content">As digital transformation becomes increasingly pervasive, personal data has emerged as a crucial component of state governance, public service delivery, digital platform operations, and the organization of social life. However, personal data protection law has largely been constructed on the assumption of a neutral, universal, and context-free data subject, and therefore fails to fully capture women’s gendered experiences in digital environments. In practice, women face a range of distinct data-related harms, including invasions of privacy online, unlawful disclosure of personal information, digital control within intimate relationships, misuse of image-based data and reproductive health data, as well as forms of discrimination reproduced through digital technologies and infrastructures. Against this backdrop, this paper examines personal data protection law through the lens of feminist law-making in order to reconsider the limitations of gender-neutral legislative thinking in Vietnam’s digital governance framework. It argues that ensuring women’s rights in digital environments cannot be reduced to the application of general data-processing principles alone. Rather, law reform must place women’s lived experiences, vulnerability, and intersecting forms of disadvantage at the centre of legal design and legal development. From this perspective, the paper explores the relationship between human rights, the right to privacy, the right to personal data protection, and the demands of gender justice in digital governance. It also evaluates the extent to which Vietnamese law responds to data-related harms that are gender-specific in nature. On that basis, the paper proposes directions for reforming personal data protection law from a feminist law-making perspective, moving from formal protection to substantive protection, from gender neutrality to gender responsiveness, and from a narrow focus on data management to a broader commitment to safeguarding women’s dignity, autonomy, and digital safety. By doing so, the paper seeks to contribute to scholarly discussions on feminist law-making in the field of digital governance, while also offering suggestions for the further development of personal data protection law in Vietnam.</p>

4505: <p class="custom-details-content">Feminist law-making must begin before the legal text is drafted: in the institutional conditions of legislative deliberation. Through an empirical analysis of Polish reproductive law debates, we show how women's voices may be formally present yet deprived of law-making agency. We therefore propose a Feminist Legislative Deliberation Project, oriented toward reconstructing not only what feminist law should say, but also how feminist law-making should listen. Inspired by the Feminist Legislation Project, we argue that, in the Polish context, a prior analytical step is necessary: the reconstruction of legislative deliberation as the space in which women's voices are either transformed into law-making agency or neutralized before they can shape legal text. This is especially important in light of the post-1989 transformation, the political authority of the Catholic Church, anti-communist moral language, and the weak institutionalization of feminist expertise. The empirical basis of the analysis is a corpus of transcripts from sessions of the Polish Sejm, parliamentary committees, and plenary sittings concerning reproductive law between 1992 and 2024, with particular attention to debates on the Act on Family Planning, Protection of the Human Foetus, and Conditions of Permissibility of Abortion. Drawing on Law and Emotions, feminist legal critique, and Dorothy Stetson's distinction between visible and invisible impact, we propose the category of elusive defeat: a situation in which women's voices are present in legislative space but systematically stripped of agency. We identify three interrelated mechanisms: the emotionalization of women's speech, procedural marginalization, and delegitimization through expertise. These mechanisms are not incidental, but form part of a legislative architecture that reproduces gendered norms of rationality and determines which voices, arguments, and experiences can shape law. On this basis, we formulate the assumptions of a Feminist Legislative Deliberation Project for Poland. We understand the rewriting of parliamentary debate as a reconstruction of the conditions of deliberation on the basis of selected fragments from Sejm and committee debates. This requires rewriting the frame of reproductive law debates, the rules of listening, the status of experience, the figure of the expert, and procedural interventions such as interruptions or withdrawals of the floor. Feminist deliberation would begin not from the question of exceptions to foetal protection, but from the state's obligations toward pregnant persons, their health, autonomy, safety, and equality. It would treat experience as legislative knowledge and require institutions to respond to women's voices rather than merely record their formal presence.</p>
