4189: <p class="custom-details-content">"This paper proposes a Latin American methodological framework for feminist legal reasoning, based on the book Perspectiva de género en la argumentación jurídica and its applied development through ARVAGE IA, an artificial intelligence tool designed for the critical analysis of legal argumentation from a gender perspective. The central claim is that gender-sensitive adjudication should not be reduced to the rhetorical invocation of equality, vulnerability, or international human rights standards. Rather, it requires a method for examining the argumentative structure of legal discourse itself: the premises it selects, the voices it excludes, the stereotypes it naturalizes, the facts it considers relevant, and the institutional silences it leaves intact. The paper engages with the conference theme of “sceptical pragmatism” by addressing a core feminist dilemma: how can law be used as a tool for emancipation while remaining alert to its androcentric, formalist, and patriarchal foundations? Instead of abandoning legal reasoning, the proposed approach seeks to reconfigure it from within. It does so through a ten-point methodological framework for legal argumentation with a gender perspective, including attention to language, critique of formal neutrality, identification of gender stereotypes, contextual and intersectional analysis, rejection of “magical formalism” and deliberate silence, protection of marginalized narratives from discursive mutilation, revision of so-called common-sense inferences, deconstruction of epistemic exclusion, identification of public policy failures, and justification of pragmatic and transformative remedies. The paper argues that feminist judgments require not only different outcomes, but also different argumentative practices. A decision may cite gender standards and still reproduce patriarchal reasoning if it fails to examine the hidden assumptions underlying its legal and factual premises. Conversely, a transformative judgment makes visible the structural conditions of inequality, incorporates the lived experiences of women and LGBTI+ persons, and justifies remedies capable of disrupting rather than merely managing gender injustice. Building on this matrix, the paper presents ARVAGE IA as a preliminary experience in translating feminist legal methodology into the field of legal artificial intelligence. ARVAGE IA does not seek to automate adjudication or replace judicial reasoning. Rather, it operates as a pedagogical and critical tool for identifying biases, stereotypes, silences, formalism, evidentiary deficits, and argumentative obstacles in legal texts. In this way, the paper contributes to feminist law-making and feminist judging by offering a situated proposal that combines feminist legal theory, Latin American judicial practice, and critical technology to evaluate whether legal discourse merely adapts to existing patriarchal frameworks or actively participates in their transformation. Keywords Feminist legal reasoning; gender-sensitive adjudication; transformative judgments; legal argumentation; legal artificial intelligence."</p>
4359: <p class="custom-details-content">This paper presents a feminist re-writing of the ECJ’s judgment in Joined Cases C-608/22 and C-609/22, AH & FN v. Federal Office for Immigration and Asylum. In the judgment, the ECJ held that the cumulative discriminatory measures imposed on women by the Taliban regime, including restrictions on education, employment, freedom of movement, and political participation, are sufficiently severe to constitute persecution under Article 9(1)(b) of the Qualification Directive. The Court further held that, for Afghan women, nationality and gender alone may suffice to establish a well-founded fear of persecution, without requiring additional individual assessments. Yet despite effectively describing an institutionalized system of domination targeting women as a group, the Court stopped short of naming that system as gender apartheid. This paper argues that the judgment already applies the logic and definition of gender apartheid while refusing to articulate it explicitly. Through a feminist judgment rewrite, I contend that naming gender apartheid is not merely symbolic, but doctrinally necessary to align EU asylum law with developments in international human rights law and international criminal law, where the codification of gender apartheid has gained momentum. Drawing on feminist legal theory, feminist judgment methodologies, and findings from the People’s Tribunal for Women of Afghanistan, this rewrite centers the testimonies and legal claims of Afghan women, who have consistently identified Taliban rule as a regime of gender apartheid (Conference Theme 4). Rather than treating persecution as a series of isolated discriminatory acts, the feminist judgment reframes the persecutor as the regime of gender apartheid itself. This reconceptualization has implications beyond Afghanistan. Grounding refugee recognition in exposure to gender apartheid instead of nationality-based premises could extend protection to those resisting structurally similar forms of state-enforced gender oppression, including Iranian women. Such an approach is especially urgent amid continuing deportations and increasingly restrictive asylum practices across the EU. The feminist rewrite therefore positions gender apartheid as a transnational category that connects distinct areas of international law. By imagining the law differently, the paper contributes to feminist law-making projects that move beyond critique toward the reconstruction of legal doctrine (Conference Theme 6). It suggests that feminist judgments can serve as tools for reshaping asylum law into a framework capable of naming and responding to gender apartheid.</p>
4497: <p class="custom-details-content">How do institutional law reform processes listen to women’s voices? Can listening with a feminist ear in law reform influence feminist lawmaking? In this paper we present findings from our investigation of listening practices in current Australian institutional law reform and policy development processes addressing violence against women. Drawing on research conducted through our Australian Research Council Discovery Project ‘Women, Listening and Law Reform’, we argue that choices in the design of law reform can increase the authority and visibility of women’s voices in law reform, but do not guarantee that they will influence recommendations or contribute to feminist lawmaking. We suggest instead that institutional law reform must attend to the listening process beyond voice, and specifically to listening with a feminist ear, to form a foundation for feminist lawmaking. Our project investigates institutional law reform processes – such as law reform commissions, parliamentary inquiries and Royal Commissions – as both a facet of, and a precursor to, formal lawmaking, with a specific focus on violence against women. We explore how such processes elicit women’s voices, particularly the voices of victim-survivors and diverse women with lived experience, and ask whether and how law reform institutions adopting feminist-informed listening practices can lead to more effective feminist lawmaking. This paper will present findings from our investigation of recent institutional law reform processes. We have identified significant variation in approaches to eliciting women’s voices, including embedding lived experience through advisory committees, partnerships with advocacy organisations and engaging women with lived experience through written submissions, roundtables and public hearings. Our research reveals that although these design choices can increase the visibility of women’s voices in law reform, they do not guarantee that their voices will be listened to in ways that influence institutional methods and frameworks. Drawing on Sara Ahmed’s theorisation of the feminist ear, we argue that law reform institutions must attend to their own listening practices and conceptualise listening as response-ability before women’s voices can contribute meaningfully to transformative change. Law reform must develop strategies for listening that dismantle colonial and gendered hierarchies, that listen for silence and for what is not and cannot be said, and that are prepared to challenge the patriarchal assumptions that underpin the legal status quo. Our paper will present key criteria for listening with a feminist ear in law reform. These criteria will underpin the development of a model for listening with a feminist ear in Phase 2 of our project, in which we will engage with prefigurative methodologies to workshop whether and how listening with a feminist ear can lead to transformative change through feminist lawmaking.</p>
4513: <p class="custom-details-content">India’s constitutional promise of equality has repeatedly been carried forward through reformist language, yet women’s presence in the institutions that make, interpret, and enforce law remains limited. The Women’s Reservation Act, 2023, marked an important constitutional moment by promising one-third reservation for women in the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies.However, the continuing uncertainty around its implementation, including the recent failure of the proposed constitutional amendment, reveals a familiar pattern: equality is formally recognised, but institutionally deferred. At the same time, women remain seriously underrepresented in the higher judiciary. Official data shows that, as of 02.02.2026, the Supreme Court had only one-woman judge, while the High Courts together had 116 women judges out of 814 working judges . This paper examines the connected exclusion of women from parliamentary law-making and higher judicial decision-making in India. Drawing on feminist legal theory, feminist constitutionalism, and the distinction between descriptive and substantive representation, it asks whether constitutional democracy can claim transformative equality when women remain marginal in both the legislature and the constitutional courts. The study adopts a doctrinal and socio-legal methodology, analysing constitutional provisions, the Women’s Reservation Act, parliamentary developments, judicial appointment data, and feminist scholarship on law-making and judging. It also engages with the conference theme of feminist law-making and judgments by treating representation not merely as numerical inclusion, but as a condition for reshaping legal reasoning, institutional legitimacy, and democratic accountability. The paper finds that India’s current model produces a gap between constitutional symbolism and institutional practice. Women’s reservation without timely implementation weakens feminist law-making, while gender imbalance in the higher judiciary narrows the possibility of feminist judging. The paper argues that feminist constitutionalism requires synchronised reform across Parliament and the judiciary, so that women are not only subjects of law, but active authors and interpreters of constitutional justice. Keywords: Feminist Law-Making; Women’s Reservation; Parliamentary Exclusion; Gender Diversity; Higher Judiciary; Feminist Constitutionalism; India.</p>
4521: <p class="custom-details-content">This paper examines the possibilities and limitations of feminist law-making in contemporary China through the lens of gender equality legislation, digital feminist activism, and the evolving relationship between state law and grassroots legal consciousness. While feminist jurisprudence has often portrayed law as a patriarchal institution that reproduces structural inequality, recent developments in China demonstrate a more complex interaction between feminist advocacy, authoritarian governance, and legal reform. The paper argues that Chinese feminist actors increasingly engage in a form of “pragmatic feminist law-making” that operates both within and beyond formal legal institutions, thereby contributing to the gradual transformation of legal discourse on gender, labor, family, and violence. The study focuses on three interrelated areas: the anti-domestic violence framework following the enactment of the Anti-Domestic Violence Law (2015), gender discrimination in employment and reproductive rights, and the rise of digital feminist mobilization surrounding sexual harassment and the \#MeToo movement in China. Rather than evaluating feminist legal advocacy solely by immediate legislative outcomes, this paper highlights the “inconspicuous impact” of feminist interventions in reshaping public legal consciousness, judicial interpretation, and administrative governance. In doing so, it engages directly with Theme 1 of the conference concerning overlooked feminist influence on legal development. At the same time, the paper addresses Theme 3 by critically assessing the limits of legal mobilization in a political environment where feminist activism faces institutional constraints and censorship. It argues that Chinese feminist actors adopt a form of “skeptical pragmatism”: they strategically utilize existing legal language, state policy discourse, and administrative complaint mechanisms while remaining aware of the patriarchal and political boundaries embedded in the legal system. This strategy allows feminists to negotiate partial reforms without fully endorsing the legitimacy of existing structures. Methodologically, the paper combines feminist legal theory with socio-legal analysis and draws upon legal texts, court cases, digital feminist campaigns, and policy debates in contemporary China. It further explores how feminist law-making can incorporate women’s lived experiences into legal discourse in contexts where formal democratic participation remains limited. Ultimately, the paper contributes to broader debates on feminist DIY law-making by demonstrating how feminist legal consciousness can emerge not only through formal legislative drafting, but also through everyday acts of interpretation, digital resistance, and grassroots legal engagement.</p>
